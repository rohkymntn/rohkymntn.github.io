Full-wave electromagnetic simulation / 20 verification tests / Python, NumPy, SciPy and Gmsh / approximately 3,000 lines / source code

Automated optimization of implantable and wearable antennas requires a full-wave validation layer capable of identifying candidate designs that satisfy surrogate objectives while violating Maxwell-consistent electromagnetic behaviour. We developed an electric-field integral-equation solver with Rao-Wilton-Glisson basis functions, validated its numerical implementation against analytical scattering and antenna solutions, isolated three sources of apparently physical but numerically spurious resonances and incorporated the solver into an iterative optimization, verification and calibration framework.

1. Motivation and design requirements

The antenna-design pipeline combines a language-model controller with parametric templates for meandered inverted-F antennas, planar inverted-F antennas, loops and patches, together with CMA-ES and Bayesian optimization, matching-network synthesis and specific absorption rate calculations. Its original objective functions depended on closed-form estimates of resonance and input impedance, including effective-permittivity corrections, height-dependent radiation resistance and parallel-RLC approximations, while the OpenEMS and NEC2 interfaces reverted to the same analytical models rather than performing full-wave simulation.

Closed-form resonance estimates with expected errors of 20 to 30 percent are appropriate for initialization but insufficient for final design verification, particularly when geometric perturbations alter current paths in ways that violate the assumptions of the surrogate model. The required solver therefore had to evaluate the pipeline's native geometries within seconds on a laptop, avoid dependence on an external electromagnetic package and report the physical approximations retained by each simulation.

2. Boundary-element formulation

The target structures consist primarily of planar copper traces, conducting plates and narrow feed or shorting pins, for which a surface integral formulation avoids volumetric discretization while incorporating the radiation condition through the Green function. Although the resulting impedance matrix is dense, antenna meshes containing several hundred to several thousand unknowns remain compatible with direct LU factorization on a workstation.

The formulation is the electric-field integral equation (EFIE) with Rao–Wilton–Glisson (RWG) basis functions on a triangular mesh. On a perfect conductor the tangential electric field vanishes, so the scattered field produced by the unknown surface current \(\mathbf J\) must cancel the tangential impressed field,

\[ \hat n \times \Big[\, j\omega\mu_0 \!\int_S \mathbf J(\mathbf r')\,G\,dS' \,-\, \frac{1}{j\omega\varepsilon_0}\nabla\!\int_S \nabla'\!\cdot\mathbf J(\mathbf r')\,G\,dS' \Big] = \hat n \times \mathbf E^{\,i}(\mathbf r), \qquad G=\frac{e^{-jkR}}{4\pi R}. \]

Expanding \(\mathbf J=\sum_n I_n \mathbf f_n\) in RWG functions (each lives on a pair of triangles sharing an edge and carries unit current across that edge) and testing with the same functions gives the dense linear system \(\mathbf Z\mathbf I=\mathbf V\) with

\[ Z_{mn}= jk\eta\left[\iint \mathbf f_m\!\cdot\!\mathbf f_n\,G\,dS'dS \,-\, \frac{1}{k^2}\iint (\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf f_m)(\nabla'\!\cdot\!\mathbf f_n)\,G\,dS'dS\right]. \]

Accurate evaluation of the double surface integrals determines the numerical stability of the formulation because the Green-function kernel is singular for coincident triangles, nearly singular for adjacent elements and smooth but oscillatory at greater separation. Inadequate treatment of these regimes produces structured spurious resonances that can be difficult to distinguish from genuine antenna modes.

4. Numerical implementation

4.1 Quadrature and the singular integrals

Triangle integration uses symmetric Dunavant quadrature rules with 1, 3, 6 and 7 barycentric points and polynomial exactness through degrees 1, 2, 4 and 5. Evaluation against analytical integrals of barycentric monomials through the corresponding degree produced agreement to approximately 10-15.

Direct quadrature of the static kernel fails when an observation point lies on or near the source triangle, motivating decomposition of the Green function into a singular static term and a smooth frequency-dependent remainder. The remainder has a finite limit as the separation approaches zero and is evaluated numerically, whereas the static contribution is integrated analytically over the triangle edges.

\[ \int_T \frac{dS'}{R} = \sum_{i=1}^{3}\left[ P^0_i \ln\frac{R^+_i+l^+_i}{R^-_i+l^-_i} \,-\, |d|\left(\tan^{-1}\frac{P^0_i l^+_i}{(R^0_i)^2+|d|R^+_i} - \tan^{-1}\frac{P^0_i l^-_i}{(R^0_i)^2+|d|R^-_i}\right)\right], \]

A companion expression supplies the vector integral required by the RWG formulation, with the logarithmic form selected according to the position of the projected observation point to avoid indeterminate evaluation along an edge extension. Comparison with adaptive double integration at five in-plane and off-plane observation points showed agreement to eight decimal places and remained finite in the singular configurations where direct numerical integration failed.

4.2 Assembling Z without loops

Assembly was expressed as sparse basis operators surrounding a dense quadrature kernel, replacing explicit loops over test triangles, source triangles and quadrature coordinates. For a sparse basis matrix \(F_c\) containing weighted Cartesian RWG components and a divergence matrix \(D\), the vector and scalar potential contributions are obtained by sparse-dense matrix products.

\[ \mathbf A=\sum_{c\in\{x,y,z\}} F_c^{\mathsf T}\,\mathbb G\,F_c,\qquad \boldsymbol\Phi = D^{\mathsf T}\,\mathbb G\, D,\qquad \mathbf Z = jk\eta\left(\mathbf A - \boldsymbol\Phi/k^2\right), \]

The kernel matrix is evaluated in row blocks and is not retained in full, while near interactions replace the static contribution with analytical basis-level integrals and subdivide the outer test triangle when element sizes differ substantially. Frequency-independent static, analytical and conductor-loss matrices are cached for each mesh, leaving only the smooth remainder to be sampled with a three-point rule at each frequency and permitting a 21-frequency sweep of an 800-unknown antenna in approximately two seconds.

Conductor loss is represented by a Leontovich surface impedance \(Z_s=(1+j)\sqrt{\omega\mu_0/2\sigma}\), halved to account for current on both faces of a conducting sheet and incorporated through the RWG Gram matrix. Complex-valued \(k\) and \(\eta\) further permit homogeneous lossy media, including muscle-equivalent tissue at 400 MHz with relative permittivity 57 and conductivity 0.8 S/m, without modification of the assembly procedure.

4.3 Ports and post-processing

Excitation is represented by a strip delta gap that assigns \(V_m=\pm V_0l_m\) to each RWG edge crossing the port, with orientation selected to produce a consistent terminal current. Sequential excitation of each port yields the admittance, impedance and scattering matrices, while integration of the radiation vector over a 37 by 73 angular grid supplies radiated power and enables direct verification of power conservation against the complex input power.

5.4 Reciprocity and energy

Automated verification further requires reciprocity within 10-3 for a two-port dipole pair separated by one sixth of a wavelength and power closure within 1 percent for a lossy copper dipole. These invariants provide sensitive tests for sign errors and normalization factors in the assembled operator.

6. Conforming meshes from parametric antenna geometries

Parametric templates represent conductors as finite-width traces, planar boxes and port definitions, which must be converted into mutually conforming surface panels before RWG connectivity can preserve current across trace intersections, plate boundaries and vertical junctions.

Mesh-convergence experiments established one transverse element for strips narrower than one fortieth of a wavelength and two elements for wider strips, plate elements no larger than one tenth of a wavelength or twice the nearest parallel-conductor spacing and gradual propagation of local refinement into adjacent panels. Junctions shared by \(k\) triangles use \(k-1\) RWG functions, which permits vertical pins to connect at arbitrary locations on a plate while preserving current continuity.

7. Numerical failure modes and correction

Application to the complete templates revealed narrow resonances at 525, 540, 570 and 640 MHz in the meandered inverted-F antenna and frequency-to-frequency impedance changes from 11 to 219 ohms in the planar inverted-F antenna, despite successful canonical validation. Controlled comparisons traced these apparently physical responses to matrix asymmetry, geometric end-cap extension and nonconforming CAD intersections.

7.1 Matrix asymmetry under symmetric factorization

Although the Galerkin EFIE operator is reciprocal and symmetric in exact arithmetic, numerical evaluation of near interactions produced relative asymmetry of approximately 10-3 because the two integration orders were not identical. Symmetric LDLT factorization silently discarded one triangle of this numerically asymmetric matrix, which amplified the discrepancy on graded meshes with condition numbers near 107 and generated spurious near-null modes.

Factorization of an identical impedance matrix by the two solvers isolated the failure, after which six-point and seven-point static quadrature rules produced a 700-ohm discrepancy under LDLT but only a 0.004-ohm discrepancy under LU. Explicit projection onto the reciprocal operator through \(\mathbf Z\leftarrow(\mathbf Z+\mathbf Z^{\mathsf T})/2\) followed by full LU factorization removed the spurious resonances.

7.2 Geometric bias from ribbon end caps

Ribbon construction initially added square end caps with half the trace width to ensure geometric overlap between connected segments. Although negligible for a 0.5-mm meander trace, this operation lengthened the 10-mm-wide validation dipole by 7 percent and shifted its resonance from 0.465 to 0.433 wavelengths, which was corrected by terminating ribbons flush and sharing the terminal edge with attached pins.

7.3 Nonconforming intersections between touching surfaces

Electrical continuity between a vertical pin and a conducting plate requires shared mesh edges, yet OpenCASCADE boolean fragmentation did not reliably imprint a face that only touched the interior of another face along an edge. The resulting geometry appeared connected in three-dimensional visualization while remaining electrically disconnected in the RWG graph.

Explicit embedding of pin boundaries connected the surfaces but duplicated geometric constraints and produced cascades of near-zero-area triangles under refinement. The final construction fragments pin endpoints into the host polygon, removes redundant embedding, applies structured transfinite meshing to ribbons, merges nodes within 10-3 element lengths and rejects meshes with minimum quality below 0.08 before retrying with an alternative algorithm, yielding stable meshes for all seven templates from refinement factors of 1.0 to 0.5.

8. Computational performance

9. Integration with multilevel optimization

The computational cost of full-wave evaluation precludes its use in every objective-function call, so the solver surrounds the analytical optimizer as a higher-fidelity verification and calibration stage.

  1. Verification. verify_design evaluates identical geometry with the analytical and full-wave models and reports resonance, target-frequency impedance and return loss, bandwidth, efficiency, gain, electrical mesh size, power balance, agreement between model fidelities and omitted physical effects. Designs containing a substrate are re-evaluated by the analytical model with unit relative permittivity so that the reported discrepancy isolates model-form error from the absent dielectric layer.
  2. Converge. A second solve on a 0.7× mesh reports how far the resonance and |Z| moved.
  3. Calibrate. The template is wrapped so its closed-form resonance and resistance are scaled to the full-wave values. Geometry, parameter bounds and optimizers are untouched, only the fast surrogate changes.
  4. Iteration. Optimization, verification and recalibration are repeated while a low-cost connectivity test penalizes feed or shorting pins that fail to intersect a conductor. This constraint was introduced after the analytical objective accepted a planar inverted-F antenna with a feed outside the patch, which the full-wave model correctly represented as an open circuit.

Five optimization and verification cycles for an air-loaded planar inverted-F antenna at 2.45 GHz showed surrogate resonance errors ranging from -44 to 17 percent across candidate geometries. The variation excluded correction by a single global scale factor and supported full-wave refinement of the highest-ranked candidate designs.

10. Electromagnetic response in lossy tissue

Far-field gain and radiation efficiency were omitted for the unbounded lossy-medium calculation because radiated power requires specification of a finite integration boundary, whereas input impedance remains well defined within the homogeneous-medium formulation.

11. Software integration

The full-wave solver implements the analytical solver interface used by the optimization objective and is available through the simulate_fullwave, verify_fullwave and fidelity_ladder agent tools, a command-line interface and the Streamlit application. Each interface reports impedance and return-loss comparisons, agreement grades, numerical diagnostics, surface-current distributions and three-dimensional gain patterns, with Gmsh initialization configured to avoid installation of signal handlers inside the Streamlit worker thread.

12. Model limitations

Homogeneous-medium approximation. The present formulation represents conductors in one homogeneous medium and therefore omits explicit dielectric interfaces. Reports estimate the expected resonance displacement using \(1-1/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{r,eff}}\), which may remain limited for electrically thin substrates but becomes substantial for patch antennas on FR-4 and motivates implementation of layered-medium Green functions.
  • Thin sheets only, thick blocks are meshed as closed shells, and closed shells carry EFIE interior resonances at discrete frequencies (a combined-field formulation would remove them).
  • Oblique 3D wires (the helix template) are refused with a message, a wire-kernel solver is the right tool there.
  • Dense O(N²) storage, about 8,000 unknowns is the practical ceiling on a laptop.
  • In a lossy medium only the input impedance is produced.

13. Planned extensions

  • Layered-medium Green function. Spectral-domain evaluation with discrete complex images would represent substrate interfaces and stratified skin, fat and muscle, enabling direct full-wave optimization of implant geometries.
  • Two-antenna links. The multi-port path already works, an implant and an external reader in one impedance matrix gives \(S_{21}\) directly, a physics-based link budget instead of Friis with assumed gains.
  • Full-wave in the loop on the optimizer's top candidates, now that the ladder has shown the surrogate's error is not a constant.
  • A compiled kernel (Numba or C++) for the fill, which is embarrassingly parallel and would push the ceiling past 20,000 unknowns.
  • Chaining into measurement. The pipeline already calibrates simulation against VNA data, the ladder should end at the bench, full-wave → measured S1P → corrected model.

14. Conclusions

The principal implementation risk was not the established EFIE formulation but the capacity of numerical and geometric errors to produce plausible resonances, impedance curves and efficiencies. Independent invariants including power conservation, reciprocity, Mie-series agreement and mesh convergence, together with experiments that varied one numerical component at a time, were required to distinguish physical behaviour from implementation artifacts and now form the verification basis of the solver.
