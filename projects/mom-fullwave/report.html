status: working, 20 tests · stack: python, numpy, scipy, gmsh · ~3,000 lines · code on github

I had an antenna-design pipeline that could optimize, but could not check its own answers. This is the story of giving it a full-wave electromagnetic solver written from scratch, validating that solver against closed-form physics, finding three bugs that each looked like physics until they were isolated, and then wrapping the solver into a loop that forces every optimizer result to answer to Maxwell's equations before an engineer sees it.

1. The problem

The pipeline (an LLM agent plus a Python library) designs small antennas for implants and wearables: meandered inverted-F antennas, PIFAs, loops, patches. It has parametric templates for each family, a CMA-ES / Bayesian optimizer, matching-network synthesis, SAR checks, and a Streamlit front end. What it did not have was physics. Every template carried a closed-form estimate of its resonance and input impedance (a quarter-wave length with an effective permittivity, a radiation resistance that scales with height squared, a parallel-RLC shape around resonance), and the optimizer ran thousands of evaluations against those formulas. The full-wave solver entries in the UI, OpenEMS and NEC2, were never wired in; selecting them printed a warning and fell back to the formulas.

The templates' own docstrings put their accuracy at ±20–30 % on resonance. For an RF engineer that is a starting point, not a deliverable. I wanted a solver that runs in seconds on a laptop, needs no external EM package, speaks the geometry the pipeline already produces, and is honest about what it does not model.

2. Choosing the method

The structures are thin conductors: copper traces on a plane, plates, vertical feed and shorting pins. A surface integral-equation method is the natural fit. Instead of discretizing a volume around the antenna (FDTD, FEM) you discretize only the metal, the radiation condition is built into the Green's function, and a few hundred unknowns describe a complete antenna. The price is a dense matrix, but for a few thousand unknowns a dense LU factorization is a fraction of a second.

The formulation is the electric-field integral equation (EFIE) with Rao–Wilton–Glisson (RWG) basis functions on a triangular mesh. On a perfect conductor the tangential electric field vanishes, so the scattered field produced by the unknown surface current \(\mathbf J\) must cancel the tangential impressed field:

\[ \hat n \times \Big[\, j\omega\mu_0 \!\int_S \mathbf J(\mathbf r')\,G\,dS' \;-\; \frac{1}{j\omega\varepsilon_0}\nabla\!\int_S \nabla'\!\cdot\mathbf J(\mathbf r')\,G\,dS' \Big] = \hat n \times \mathbf E^{\,i}(\mathbf r), \qquad G=\frac{e^{-jkR}}{4\pi R}. \]

Expanding \(\mathbf J=\sum_n I_n \mathbf f_n\) in RWG functions (each lives on a pair of triangles sharing an edge and carries unit current across that edge) and testing with the same functions gives the dense linear system \(\mathbf Z\mathbf I=\mathbf V\) with

\[ Z_{mn}= jk\eta\left[\iint \mathbf f_m\!\cdot\!\mathbf f_n\,G\,dS'dS \;-\; \frac{1}{k^2}\iint (\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf f_m)(\nabla'\!\cdot\!\mathbf f_n)\,G\,dS'dS\right]. \]

Everything difficult about the method is in evaluating those double integrals: the kernel is singular when the two triangles coincide, nearly singular when they touch, and smooth but oscillatory when they are far apart. Get that wrong and you do not get slightly wrong answers; you get spurious resonances that look exactly like real ones.

4. Building the numerics, bottom up

4.1 Quadrature and the singular integrals

Integration over triangles uses symmetric Gauss (Dunavant) rules in barycentric coordinates: 1, 3, 6 and 7 points, exact to polynomial degree 1, 2, 4 and 5. The first test I wrote checks every rule against \(\int_T \lambda_1^a\lambda_2^b\,dA = a!\,b!\,2A/(a+b+2)!\) for all monomials up to its degree; they pass to 10⁻¹⁵.

Sampling \(1/R\) with any such rule fails when the observation point is on or near the source triangle. So the kernel is split, \(G = \frac{1}{4\pi R} + \frac{e^{-jkR}-1}{4\pi R}\): the second term is smooth everywhere (its limit at \(R\to0\) is \(-jk/4\pi\)) and can be sampled; the first is integrated in closed form. For an observation point \(\mathbf r\) at signed height \(d\) above the plane of triangle \(T\), with \(P^0_i\) the signed distance from the projection of \(\mathbf r\) to edge \(i\), \(l^\pm_i\) the signed positions of the edge endpoints along the edge, and \(R^\pm_i\) the distances to them,

\[ \int_T \frac{dS'}{R} = \sum_{i=1}^{3}\left[ P^0_i \ln\frac{R^+_i+l^+_i}{R^-_i+l^-_i} \;-\; |d|\left(\tan^{-1}\frac{P^0_i l^+_i}{(R^0_i)^2+|d|R^+_i} - \tan^{-1}\frac{P^0_i l^-_i}{(R^0_i)^2+|d|R^-_i}\right)\right], \]

with a companion formula for the vector integral \(\int_T (\mathbf r'-\boldsymbol\rho)/R\,dS'\). The logarithm has two algebraically equal forms, \(\ln\frac{R^++l^+}{R^-+l^-}\) and \(\ln\frac{R^--l^-}{R^+-l^+}\), and only one of them is numerically stable depending on which side of the edge the point projects to; picking the wrong one gives 0/0 for points on the edge's extension. I tested the implementation against adaptive double integration at five observation points (inside, above, outside in-plane, off-plane) to eight digits, and it stays finite where the numerical integral diverges.

4.2 Assembling Z without loops

Written naively, the fill is a quadruple loop over test and source triangles and their quadrature points. I wrote it instead as sparse matrices around one dense kernel. If \(F_c\) is the sparse matrix whose entry \([(t,i),n]\) holds \(W_{ti}\,f_n(\mathbf r_{ti})_c\) (quadrature weight times the c-component of basis n at point i of triangle t) and \(D\) holds \(W_{ti}\,\nabla\!\cdot\!\mathbf f_n\), then

\[ \mathbf A=\sum_{c\in\{x,y,z\}} F_c^{\mathsf T}\,\mathbb G\,F_c,\qquad \boldsymbol\Phi = D^{\mathsf T}\,\mathbb G\, D,\qquad \mathbf Z = jk\eta\left(\mathbf A - \boldsymbol\Phi/k^2\right), \]

where \(\mathbb G\) is the kernel sampled between all pairs of quadrature points. That matrix is computed in row blocks and never stored whole. The near pairs (centroid distance under three element sizes) have their \(1/R\) part zeroed in \(\mathbb G\) and replaced by the closed-form integrals at the basis level; when the test triangle is much larger than the source triangle, the test triangle is subdivided into 4, 16 or 64 sub-triangles for that outer integral because the inner potential is sharply peaked there. Everything frequency-independent (the static kernel, the analytic near terms, the Gram matrix for conductor loss) is computed once per mesh; per frequency only the smooth remainder is sampled, with a cheaper 3-point rule. A 21-point sweep on an 800-unknown antenna takes about two seconds.

Conductor loss enters as a Leontovich surface impedance \(Z_s=(1+j)\sqrt{\omega\mu_0/2\sigma}\), halved because a sheet carries current on both faces, added to the diagonal blocks through the same Gram matrix. Because \(k\) and \(\eta\) are complex throughout, a lossy homogeneous background medium (muscle at 400 MHz: εr 57, σ 0.8 S/m) costs nothing extra.

4.3 Ports and post-processing

Feeds are strip delta gaps: a line across a conductor where a 1 V source is impressed. Every RWG edge lying on that line gets \(V_m=\pm V_0 l_m\), with the sign chosen so all of them push current in the port direction, and the terminal current is \(\sum \pm I_m l_m\). For multi-port structures each port is driven in turn with the others shorted, which gives the Y matrix directly, then Z and S. The far field comes from the radiation vector \(\mathbf N(\hat r)=\sum_i W_i\mathbf J_i e^{jk\hat r\cdot\mathbf r_i}\) on a 37 × 73 sphere grid; integrating the radiation intensity gives \(P_{rad}\), and comparing it with \(\tfrac12\mathrm{Re}(V_0 I^*)\) gives a power-balance number that every run reports. For a lossless conductor it must be 1.000, and the solver prints it precisely so that a user never has to trust a number they cannot check.

5.2 Scattering from a sphere, against the Mie series

The dipole exercises the feed model as much as the matrix. To test the matrix alone I meshed a PEC sphere with Gmsh, illuminated it with a plane wave (the excitation vector is then just \(F^{\mathsf T}\) applied to the incident field samples) and compared the backscatter cross-section with the exact Mie series, which I implemented from spherical Bessel functions in a dozen lines. The two agree within 0.35 dB across ka from 0.6 to 3 on a λ/10 mesh (1,068 unknowns), including through the resonance-region oscillations.

5.4 Reciprocity and energy

Two more tests run on every commit: a two-port pair of dipoles at λ/6 spacing must give \(S_{21}=S_{12}\) to 10⁻³, and a copper dipole must satisfy \((P_{rad}+P_{loss})/P_{in}=1\) to 1 % while radiating less than 100 % of its input. These are cheap, and they are exactly the invariants that break first when a sign or a factor of two goes wrong in the fill.

6. From templates to meshes

The pipeline's templates emit traces (start, end, width), boxes (ground planes, patches, substrates) and ports. Turning that into a mesh a boundary-element solver can use is where most of the engineering went.

Meshing rules that came out of the design/test cycles: one element across a strip narrower than λ/40, two across wider ones; plates at λ/10 but never coarser than twice the spacing to the nearest parallel conductor (a patch 5 mm above ground meshed at 10 mm gave nonsense); grading fields from every fine panel into its coarse neighbours so element sizes never jump by more than about 2×; and the k−1 rule for junction edges so a pin can meet a plate anywhere on its surface.

7. Three bugs that looked like physics

The validation cases passed on the first try. The templates did not: the MIFA showed a comb of narrow resonances at 525, 540, 570 and 640 MHz, far below its first physical resonance, and the PIFA's impedance jumped between 11 Ω and 219 Ω from one 50 MHz step to the next. Each of the three causes took a controlled experiment to find.

7.1 The symmetric solve

The Galerkin EFIE matrix is symmetric in exact arithmetic (reciprocity), so I had used SciPy's symmetric LDLᵀ path. But the numerical matrix is only symmetric to about 10⁻³, because near-pair integrals are evaluated with the test integral on one triangle and the analytic integral on the other, and the two orderings do not produce identical numbers. An LDLᵀ solver reads only one triangle of the matrix and silently assumes the other. On a well-conditioned mesh the 10⁻³ inconsistency is harmless; on a graded mesh with condition number 10⁷ it is amplified into spurious near-null vectors, which show up as resonances.

The experiment that found it: identical matrix, two solvers. Then I confirmed that a 6-point and a 7-point static quadrature rule, which differ only in their asymmetry error, gave impedances differing by 700 Ω with LDLᵀ and by 0.004 Ω with LU. The fix is two lines: symmetrize \(\mathbf Z\leftarrow(\mathbf Z+\mathbf Z^{\mathsf T})/2\), which is legitimate because the true operator is reciprocal, then a full LU.

7.2 The end caps

When the layout code turned a trace into a ribbon polygon, it added square caps of half a width at each end so that touching chains would overlap and fuse. On a 0.5 mm MIFA trace that is 0.25 mm and invisible. On the 10 mm-wide validation dipole it is 5 mm at each end, 7 % more length, and the Gmsh-meshed dipole resonated at 0.433 λ instead of 0.465 λ. I spent an afternoon suspecting the unstructured mesh. The fix was to make ribbons flush and let pins share the ribbon's end edge instead.

7.3 The CAD kernel does not imprint touching faces

A vertical pin meeting a plate must share mesh edges with it or current cannot flow. I relied on OpenCASCADE's boolean fragment to imprint the pin's edge into the plate face. It does so for lines, and for faces that cross, but not reliably for a face that merely touches another along an edge inside it. The pin looked connected in a 3D view and was electrically floating.

The first fix, explicitly embedding the pin's boundary curves into the host face, connected the pin but created a new failure at finer mesh sizes: the mesher, given the same constraint twice (a fragmented line and an embedded curve at the same location, plus a 1D node that happened to land on the pin's corner), produced cascades of zero-area triangles that halved in size at each step. The final fix fragments the pin's end edges as explicit lines and inserts their endpoints as vertices of the host polygon, removes the embed step entirely, meshes ribbons with structured transfinite grids, merges nodes closer than 10⁻³ of an element, and rejects any mesh whose minimum triangle quality \(4\sqrt3 A/\sum l^2\) falls under 0.08 before retrying with another algorithm. All seven templates now mesh cleanly from refinement 1.0 down to 0.5.

9. Closing the loop: the fidelity ladder

With a solver I could trust, the question became how to use it. It is too slow to sit inside the optimizer's thousands of evaluations, so I put it around the optimizer instead.

  1. Verify. verify_design runs the closed-form model and the full-wave solver on the same geometry and produces a report: full-wave resonances, S11 and impedance at target, bandwidth, efficiency, gain, mesh size in wavelengths, power balance, a graded agreement, and a list of what was not modelled. The comparison is like-for-like: if the design has a substrate, the closed-form model is re-evaluated with εr = 1 so the discrepancy measures model error rather than the missing dielectric.
  2. Converge. A second solve on a 0.7× mesh reports how far the resonance and |Z| moved.
  3. Calibrate. The template is wrapped so its closed-form resonance and resistance are scaled to the full-wave values. Geometry, parameter bounds and optimizers are untouched; only the fast surrogate changes.
  4. Repeat. Optimize on the calibrated surrogate, verify, recalibrate. A millisecond connectivity check (does every pin end land on a conductor, does the port cut one) runs inside the optimizer as a penalty, because the first ladder run caught the optimizer placing the PIFA's feed pin outside the patch, a design the closed-form model was happy with and the full-wave solver correctly saw as an open circuit.

The ladder surfaced something more useful than a scale factor. Running it five times on an air PIFA at 2.45 GHz, the closed-form model's error swung from −44 % to +17 % between candidate designs. The error is not a constant, so no global calibration can remove it, and the ladder now says so in its output and reports the best verified round instead of the last one. The honest conclusion is that the final refinement of a design belongs to the full-wave solver, which is the first item on the roadmap below.

10. Implants: what happens in tissue

Far field and efficiency are deliberately not reported in a lossy medium: radiated power is not defined without a boundary to integrate over, and printing a number there would be a lie.

11. Where it lives

The solver implements the same interface as the pipeline's analytical solver, so it drops into the optimizer's objective unchanged. It is exposed three ways: as agent tools (simulate_fullwave, verify_fullwave, fidelity_ladder, with the system prompt now making verification a mandatory phase), as a CLI (python app.py verify -t pifa -f 2000), and in the Streamlit UI, where selecting the full-wave solver shows the S11 overlay against the closed-form model, the graded agreement, the diagnostics, the surface-current map on the mesh, and the full-wave 3D gain pattern. Gmsh installs its own signal handlers, which fail inside a Streamlit worker thread; that was a one-argument fix once found.

12. Limits, stated plainly

No dielectrics. The solver models conductors in a homogeneous medium. A substrate-loaded design resonates lower in reality by roughly \(1-1/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{r,eff}}\), and every report prints that range. For a thin substrate under an antenna in air this is a mild error; for a patch on FR-4 it is not, and the tool should be read as air-substrate physics until layered-media Green's functions are implemented.
  • Thin sheets only; thick blocks are meshed as closed shells, and closed shells carry EFIE interior resonances at discrete frequencies (a combined-field formulation would remove them).
  • Oblique 3D wires (the helix template) are refused with a message; a wire-kernel solver is the right tool there.
  • Dense O(N²) storage; about 8,000 unknowns is the practical ceiling on a laptop.
  • In a lossy medium only the input impedance is produced.

13. What I would build next

  • Layered-media Green's function (spectral-domain, discrete complex images) for substrates and skin/fat/muscle stacks. This removes the biggest caveat and is the difference between a verification tool and a design tool for implants.
  • Two-antenna links. The multi-port path already works; an implant and an external reader in one impedance matrix gives \(S_{21}\) directly, a physics-based link budget instead of Friis with assumed gains.
  • Full-wave in the loop on the optimizer's top candidates, now that the ladder has shown the surrogate's error is not a constant.
  • A compiled kernel (Numba or C++) for the fill, which is embarrassingly parallel and would push the ceiling past 20,000 unknowns.
  • Chaining into measurement. The pipeline already calibrates simulation against VNA data; the ladder should end at the bench, full-wave → measured S1P → corrected model.

14. What I took from it

The physics was the easy part; the classical formulation has been settled since the 1980s and it worked on the first validation case. The hard part, and the part I would not have believed without doing it, is that every one of the failures I hit produced output that looked like antenna physics: resonances at plausible frequencies, impedance curves with the right shape, efficiencies between 0 and 1. The only things that separated a bug from a result were invariants I could compute independently (power balance, reciprocity, Mie, refinement convergence) and experiments that changed one thing at a time. A solver without those is not a solver.
