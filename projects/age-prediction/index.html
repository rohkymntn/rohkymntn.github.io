Molecular age prediction: multimodal integration and few-shot learning

Iteration 1 / Blood methylation and microbiomeIteration 2 / Few-shot adaptation

Iteration 1Blood methylation and microbiome integration

Abstract

A clock-preserving residual model reduced age-prediction MAE from 9.53 to 4.85 years relative to early clock–microbiome feature fusion in 123 paired participants. Performance was comparable to calibrated methylation scores alone; incremental microbial benefit was not detected. This exploratory result motivates evaluating how fusion design affects retention of a strong unimodal predictor.

Principal empirical finding. Integration of skin microbial features with study, body-site, and sex information reduced participant-weighted age-prediction error from 9.71 to 8.74 years (10.0% reduction). The paired improvement was 0.97 years (95% conditional bootstrap interval, 0.66–1.28), with a positive average difference in each of three repeated nested-validation runs. This result concerns microbiome–metadata integration in the skin cohort; it is not an estimate of blood–skin fusion accuracy.

DNA methylation and skin microbial composition provide distinct measurements of age-associated biological variation. This project implements a prediction-level integration framework comprising a blood methylation regressor, a skin microbiome regressor, and a late-fusion operator. Public datasets included 360 discovery blood samples, 274 external blood samples, and 1,798 skin samples representing 339 participant identifiers. Nested validation yielded mean absolute errors (MAEs) of 4.21 years for blood methylation and 9.17 years for skin microbiome prediction. External blood validation yielded an MAE of 6.03 years. The modality-specific regressors were fitted on Modal and combined through a prespecified equal-weight prediction rule. Because the available cohorts do not contain verified paired blood and skin specimens, joint predictive accuracy and improvement over single-modality models remain unestimated.

The primary reduction was 0.97 years (95% conditional interval, 0.66–1.28); the secondary reduction was 0.41 years (0.10–0.74). The aggregate benefit was therefore driven primarily by study 10317, which contributed 237 of 339 participant identifiers; it was not a uniform improvement across studies.

Paired methylation-clock and gut microbiome pilot

A public paired-cohort extension used the HI-SEED released data: 123 participants with exactly matched metadata and microbial-table identifiers. Across three repeated five-fold validations, calibrated Horvath scores achieved MAE = 4.87 years, genus profiles achieved 12.59 years, and their early feature fusion achieved 9.53 years. The primary paired reduction relative to the clock was −4.66 years (95% conditional interval, −6.03 to −3.35). Thus, the tested fusion model degraded performance relative to the stronger unimodal comparator. Prior clock-training overlap has not been established, and there is no external validation. These results do not evaluate raw methylome–skin fusion.

Clock-preserving residual fusion limits loss of predictive accuracy

To preserve the stronger methylation-clock predictor, a subsequent model left clock calibration unpenalized and learned a separate correction from clock-orthogonalized microbial features. In the same repeated outer partitions, protected residual fusion achieved MAE = 4.848 years, compared with 4.851 for inner-selected calibration alone and 4.870 for OLS calibration. Relative to the earlier feature-fusion implementation (9.529 years), the post hoc paired reduction was 4.68 years (95% conditional interval, 3.42–6.07), a 49.1% reduction in MAE.

The prespecified comparison against selected calibration alone yielded a reduction of 0.004 years (95% conditional interval, −0.061 to 0.070). Microbial correction was selected in 12 of 15 outer training partitions, but this selection did not translate into a detectable held-out advantage. The supported engineering result is recovery of clock-level accuracy after the degradation caused by early fusion. Neither superiority over the stronger clock baseline nor independent biological complementarity is established.

Sampling structure and external validity

Random sample splitting placed participants represented in the training set in 90.5% of test samples. Participant and study holdout eliminated this overlap. For the fixed forest, participant-weighted MAE increased from 8.93 years under random splitting to 9.24 years under participant holdout. The paired difference was 0.31 years (95% conditional interval, 0.15–0.48). For identical participant-held-out predictions, sample-weighted MAE was 6.44 years, demonstrating sensitivity to the evaluation weights.

Leave-one-study-out MAE was 18.35 years. Study 1841 predominantly included younger adults, whereas study 10317 covered a broader and older population. Differences in age distribution and study composition are therefore plausible contributors to the reduced transfer performance. These analyses use different eligibility criteria, weighting, transformations, and model settings from the source publication and do not constitute a direct reproduction of its reported accuracy.

Assay quality and feature interpretation

Two external blood samples had more than 1% missing CpG measurements. The most incomplete sample had approximately 57% missing measurements and a predicted age of −28.4 years at recorded age 74. Both samples were retained in the primary analysis. An exploratory 1% missingness filter applied to the existing predictions retained 272 controls and yielded MAE = 5.48 years and R² = −0.26. This post hoc sensitivity analysis identifies a quality-control concern without establishing external validity.

CpGs annotated to NHLRC1, SCGN, and ASPA had large standardized ridge coefficients and were selected in all five outer training sets. These conditional coefficients do not identify causal aging mechanisms. Microbial training-importance ranks included ASVs assigned to Mycoplasma and Enterobacteriaceae; interpretation is limited by feature correlation, potential study effects, and the resolution of the released taxonomic annotations.

Matched unshuffled models had lower MAE than the ten corresponding label-permutation runs in each modality. This diagnostic supports learnable age-associated structure in the selected datasets, but the number of permutations is insufficient for a precise significance estimate.

Exploratory pathway analysis

The 500 selected methylation probes mapped to 472 unique genes. Reactome analysis tested 1,333 pathways against a background of 14,475 genes represented by the measured array. No pathway passed Benjamini–Hochberg correction at FDR < 0.05. The nominal annotations shown in Figure 3 therefore define hypotheses for follow-up rather than established pathway-level aging signatures. Full results, including all tested pathways, are available in the enrichment table.

Discussion

A proposed expansion evaluates predictive complementarity, cross-omic biological interpretation, and health associations within a single paired-cohort design. The research protocol and dataset audit distinguish completed analyses from proposed experiments and document access requirements.

The principal positive result is the incremental age-prediction information obtained by modeling microbial features jointly with observed metadata. The improvement persisted across three participant-held-out validation repetitions and exceeded both specified comparators in aggregate. The study-stratified results delimit that conclusion: the benefit depended on source-study composition, and the resampling intervals do not incorporate uncertainty from retraining or an unseen external population.

This implementation establishes the two fitted component regressors and prediction-level fusion architecture for multimodal age prediction. The component results support age prediction within the selected datasets, with weaker performance under external evaluation. They do not establish that fusion improves accuracy.

For component errors eb and es, fusion error variance satisfies:

Var(efusion) = w² Var(eb) + (1 − w)² Var(es) + 2w(1 − w) Cov(eb, es).

The unpaired cohorts do not identify the within-person covariance term or joint bias. Consequently, separate component error estimates cannot be combined to infer empirical fusion performance. A paired cohort would permit weight estimation on training or calibration data and comparison of the locked fusion model against both components on the same untouched test participants.

Additional limitations include processed-input dependence, ambiguity in a subset of source identifiers, a restricted external age range, potential assay and study effects, and the distinction between chronological and biological age. The discovery cohorts remain insufficient to establish generalizable multimodal or clinical utility.

Methods

Clock-preserving residual models

Within each training partition, clock calibration was selected among ordinary least squares, Huber regression, and unpenalized median regression. Microbial Hellinger features were residualized against the clock using training-only multivariate linear regression. A linear or RBF kernel ridge model then predicted training age residuals after clock calibration. RBF γ was fixed at 1; α ∈ {0.1, 1, 10} and correction strength ∈ {0.25, 1} were compared with a no-correction fallback. Three-fold inner MAE selected configurations, nested within the same three five-fold participant partitions used previously. Model families and the primary contrast were recorded before this experiment. Because the cohort had already been analyzed, this remains exploratory model development. The bootstrap treats each participant’s repeated-prediction loss as fixed and does not incorporate retraining or prior adaptive research choices. Full results and conditional sign-flip sensitivity tests are included in the model summary.

Paired clock-score pilot

Horvath scores were calibrated by linear regression in each training fold. Genus abundances underwent sample-local Hellinger transformation. Microbiome and concatenated clock–microbiome ridge models used training-fold standardization, with α ∈ {0.1, 1, 10, 100, 1000} selected by three-fold inner validation for MAE. Three seeded five-fold outer partitions kept participants separate. Clinical outcomes, age-acceleration residuals and the ambiguously scaled GrimAge2 column were excluded from prediction inputs. Conditional intervals resampled 123 participants 10,000 times after averaging each participant’s absolute losses across repeats. The specification and source-code hash were recorded before execution; all model summaries are available.

Datasets and eligibility criteria

Blood methylation discovery data were obtained from GSE41037. Of 720 deposited samples, 360 were retained after restricting to healthy controls aged at least 18 years with a positive source quality-control flag. External validation used 274 control women from GSE19711. Both processed matrices contained 27,578 CpG identifiers. The external target was age at recruitment, which may differ from age at blood collection.

Skin microbial abundances and metadata were obtained from the Huang et al. repository. The released matrix contained 1,975 samples and 7,311 amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Study 11052 was excluded because all 177 samples shared one participant identifier despite discrepant age values. The retained dataset comprised 1,798 samples and 339 study-qualified participant identifiers across three studies. Thirteen retained identifiers had one-year differences in recorded age across samples; all samples associated with an identifier remained in the same validation fold.

Skin observations were weighted inversely by the number of samples per participant during fitting and primary evaluation. This prevented participants with repeated sampling from contributing disproportionate weight. Dataset composition and age distributions are reported in Figure 1. The source microbial matrix had undergone age-independent prevalence filtering before release; that upstream processing could not be reconstructed from the available table.

Modality-specific age prediction

The methylation pipeline comprised median imputation, age-correlation feature selection, standardization, and ridge regression. Five outer folds held out complete assay plates. Three-fold plate-grouped inner validation selected 100 or 500 CpGs and ridge regularization parameters α ∈ {10, 100, 1000}. Every preprocessing operation was fitted within the relevant training partition. A final discovery-only selection identified 500 CpGs and α = 100; the resulting model was applied to the external controls without age-based recalibration.

The microbiome pipeline applied a sample-local Hellinger transformation, equivalent to the square root of relative abundance, and a training-only prevalence threshold of 1%. Random forests contained 160 trees with square-root feature subsampling. Minimum leaf sizes of 1, 3, and 5 were compared using three-fold participant-grouped inner validation within five outer participant-grouped folds. A minimum leaf size of 1 was selected in every outer fold and used for the final component refit. Final refitting does not provide an additional estimate of prediction accuracy.

Late-fusion architecture

The integrated estimator combines the component predictions for a shared participant:

ŷfusion = w ŷblood + (1 − w) ŷskin,   w = 0.5.

Equal weighting is a prespecified implementation choice. It was not optimized using the unpaired cohorts. The inference interface requires matched participant indices, documented specimen linkage, and the fitted feature schemas. Missing, duplicated, or discordant participant identifiers are rejected. Independent subjects are not matched by age or by row order. Separate blood-only and skin-only inference methods are available when only one modality is measured.

The model artifact contains both fitted regressors, their feature identifiers, and the fusion operator. Fitting was executed on Modal using four CPU cores; the selected estimators did not require GPU execution. Serialization checks confirmed preservation of predictions. The refitted blood model differed from the previously recorded external predictions by at most 0.00014 years, consistent with numerical differences between compute environments. No paired test participants were available, so the model manifest records joint accuracy as unestimated.

Validation and statistical analysis

Training-age median baselines were evaluated for both modalities. A skin metadata-only ridge model used study, body site, and sex. A fixed random forest with minimum leaf size 3 was evaluated using random-sample, participant-held-out, and leave-one-study-out splits to assess the effects of sampling structure. Ten label-permutation runs per modality were compared with matched unshuffled models; skin permutation operated on participant-mean ages.

MAE, root mean squared error, and R² were computed from held-out predictions. MAE intervals used 2,000 bootstrap resamples of assay plates for internal blood validation, samples for external blood validation, and participants for skin validation. These intervals condition on fitted predictions and do not include uncertainty from retraining. The random seed was 20260905. Comparisons across modalities are descriptive because the cohorts differ in demographic composition and age range.

Repeated evaluation of microbiome–metadata integration

An exploratory follow-up evaluated whether microbial features improve prediction beyond study, body site, and sex. The experiment specification and source-code hash were recorded before execution. All three specified seeds (20260906, 20260907, and 20260908), all comparisons, and all source studies were retained. Because the cohorts had already been analyzed, this is a follow-up analysis rather than independent external confirmation.

The combined estimator comprised a metadata ridge model (α = 10) and a random forest fitted to its training residuals using microbial features. Their predictions were added at inference. One-hot encoding, prevalence filtering, residual construction, and all fitting were repeated inside each training partition. The forest used 160 trees and inner selection among minimum leaf sizes of 1, 3, and 8. The metadata comparator was selected by inner validation between ridge regression and a 160-tree metadata forest with minimum leaf size 10 and all metadata features available at each split. A separately tuned microbial forest provided the secondary comparator.

Each repetition used five outer participant-grouped folds and three inner participant-grouped folds. The primary endpoint was the participant-weighted MAE reduction versus the inner-selected metadata model; reduction versus microbial prediction alone was secondary. Absolute errors were averaged within participant across repeated samples and the three repetitions. Conditional intervals used 10,000 paired participant bootstrap resamples stratified by source study. This analysis measures predictive information within the sampled study mixture and does not establish transfer to an unseen study.

Iteration 2Few-shot adaptation across methylation cohorts

The second iteration investigates adaptation under limited target-cohort labels, following the multimodal experiments above. Both iterations retain their complete results, methods and reproducibility files.

Abstract

Transporting methylation age predictors between populations and assays can require adaptation with limited labelled data. We evaluated an episodically trained neural representation with a differentiable ridge-regression adaptation step across two external cohorts. Source training used 542 samples, with 92 separate samples for checkpoint selection. External evaluation used 588 fixed query participants, disjoint from support pools supplying 8, 16 or 32 labelled examples. With 16 labels, the augmented neural model achieved mean absolute errors of 7.293 years in whole blood and 3.670 years in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), compared with 7.620 and 4.053 years for classical baselines selected by support-only cross-validation. Conditional paired reductions were 0.326 years (95% interval 0.185–0.472; Holm-adjusted P = 0.0002) and 0.384 years (0.211–0.573; P = 0.0002). However, frozen source ridge and a subsequent assay-matched source-refitting diagnostic outperformed the neural model in whole blood, while affine recalibration performed similarly in PBMCs. The findings support a limited advantage over the specified small-support model-selection procedure, without establishing superiority over the strongest individual baseline or a novel aging mechanism.

Results

Source-domain learning and independent external evaluation

Healthy adult control samples from GSE41037 and control women from GSE19711 provided 634 source samples. Discovery plate H (92 samples) was reserved for source-only checkpoint selection; the remaining 542 samples supplied feature selection, preprocessing, source-clock fitting and neural training. The model used 1,024 CpGs selected exclusively in the source-training partition. The external whole-blood cohort GSE40279 contained 656 participants aged 19–101 years. The PBMC release GSE37008 contained 99 deposited arrays; 92 participants aged 24–45 years remained after the specified quality filters and identifier audit.

Fixed random support pools contained 128 whole-blood participants and 32 PBMC participants, leaving 528 and 60 query participants. Query participants never entered model fitting, support-only baseline selection or checkpoint selection. Twenty seeded support draws were evaluated at each label budget, with the 16-shot comparison specified as primary. The target releases contained observations for 834 and 809 of the 1,024 selected probes; entirely unavailable probes were imputed using source-training medians.

Improvement relative to support-selected classical adaptation

The augmented neural adapter reduced 16-shot MAE by 4.3% in whole blood and 9.5% in PBMCs relative to the baseline family selected within each support set. Both primary contrasts passed Holm correction across the two external cohorts under the specified conditional sign-flip analysis. These comparisons concern expected error across the recorded support draws on the fixed query sets; they do not establish significance after arbitrary future model revisions or in a broader population of cohorts.

With only eight labels, whole-blood neural MAE was lower than the support-selected baseline; the difference narrowed as the support budget increased. PBMC neural predictions remained close to affine recalibration across budgets. The complete 16-shot comparison is reported below, including methods that outperformed the neural model.

Individual baselines delimit the neural-model advantage

In whole blood, frozen source ridge achieved MAE = 6.842 years, lower than the augmented neural model’s 7.293 years. Linear and RBF residual kernels also had lower point estimates (7.038 and 7.052 years). In PBMCs, affine recalibration achieved 3.854 years; the neural reduction relative to affine recalibration was 0.185 years, with a conditional interval spanning zero. Consequently, the positive primary results cannot be interpreted as uniformly better prediction than the best classical comparator. Support-set model selection itself contributes uncertainty and can choose a suboptimal adaptation method when very few labels are available.

Augmentation and assay-availability diagnostics

Removing synthetic feature augmentation slightly improved the neural point estimates in both cohorts: MAEs were 7.266 years in whole blood and 3.656 years in PBMCs. The current experiment therefore does not support a predictive advantage from its augmentation mechanism. Training traces showed that later checkpoints could perform worse on the source-validation domain, reinforcing the importance of source-only checkpoint selection.

After the primary results were inspected, we added a diagnostic that refit source ridge using only probes observed in each target release. Regularization was selected using source-group cross-validation; target ages did not enter fitting or tuning. This assay-matched source model achieved MAE = 6.094 years in whole blood and 7.893 years in PBMCs. An additional post hoc offset-only correction achieved MAEs of 7.088 and 4.020 years, respectively. These divergent results show that neither probe availability nor a simple cohort-mean shift resolves all forms of tissue and cohort shift. Because this diagnostic was added after evaluation, it is labelled post hoc and is not presented as an independently validated new method.

Discussion

This cycle tested a new model family on external query cohorts without returning to the earlier fusion objective. The neural meta-adapter improved upon a prespecified support-only model-selection procedure in both cohorts, but stronger individual baselines limit the scope of that result. The evidence supports further investigation of adaptation reliability and baseline selection under label scarcity. It does not support a claim of state-of-the-art age prediction, a newly discovered biological pathway, or established methodological novelty.

A productive next model would need to estimate when adaptation is likely to harm a strong source predictor and account for the interaction between available CpGs and population shift. The present external results may inform that development, but doing so would turn these cohorts into development evidence; a new independent query cohort would then be required for confirmation. The existing results and model configurations should remain fixed in the experiment record.

Methods

Preprocessing and source prediction

GSE37008 reports normalized M-values, as documented in its sample-table definition. These were converted to beta values using β = 1/(1 + 2−M) before source scaling. An initial PBMC evaluation incorrectly used M-values directly and was invalidated during the final scale audit. The corrected evaluation retained every model weight, hyperparameter grid and support/query partition. The correction record and input-scale audit document this replacement. Only corrected PBMC results appear here.

Source-training medians imputed missing measurements. Age-correlation screening selected 1,024 eligible CpGs, followed by source-training standardization and clipping at ±8 standard deviations. Source ridge regularization was chosen from α ∈ {10, 100, 1000} by group-held-out source validation. Feature selection preceded this internal alpha comparison; its development scores are therefore not unbiased generalization estimates. The external query comparisons remain independent of that selection. Source-training neural episodes used group-out-of-fold clock predictions to construct residual targets. GSE19711 ages refer to recruitment rather than necessarily the exact blood-collection date.

Differentiable few-shot adaptation

The encoder comprised linear layers of dimensions 1,024 → 128 → 32 with LayerNorm, GELU and tanh. Its output was concatenated with a learned scaling of the source-clock prediction. For support embeddings H and source-clock residuals r, centered kernel ridge regression fitted a residual correction with a learned positive regularization parameter. The query prediction was the source-clock estimate plus the support-fitted residual intercept and kernel correction. Gradients through the linear solve trained the encoder, regularization and clock-coordinate scale.

Each of six T4 GPU runs used 2,000 source-domain episodes and AdamW, with three seeds per augmentation condition. Episode support and query samples were disjoint. Augmentation applied task-level Gaussian shifts, multiplicative scales and feature masking to encoder inputs while keeping the supplied source-clock values fixed. Twelve fixed 16-shot episodes from the source-validation plate selected checkpoints every 100 training steps. Predictions were ensembled over the three seeds within each condition.

Classical comparators and statistical analysis

The baseline family comprised unchanged source ridge, support median, affine recalibration, target-only ridge, linear residual kernel adaptation and RBF residual kernel adaptation. Kernel regularization used α ∈ {0.01, 0.1, 1, 10}; four-fold cross-validation within each support set selected hyperparameters and the primary classical comparator. All methods used identical support/query partitions and source-selected input features. The fixed external query labels were accessed only for evaluation.

For each query participant, absolute errors were averaged over 20 support draws. Ten thousand paired participant bootstrap samples supplied conditional intervals; conditional two-sided sign-flip tests supplied P values. Sign-flip inference assumes exchangeability of signed paired losses. Holm correction covered the two primary cohort contrasts, with secondary contrasts corrected as a separate exploratory family. Shared support pools, source-model retraining uncertainty and uncertainty from selecting the fixed support pool are not captured by these intervals. At 32 shots in PBMCs, the complete 32-person pool is reused; only support-CV assignments vary. Two external cohorts do not establish general population-wide validity.
