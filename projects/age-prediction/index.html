Integration of blood methylation and skin microbiome data for age prediction

W3PHIAI 2023 concept · computational implementation, September 2026 · Python / scikit-learn / Modal

Abstract

DNA methylation and skin microbial composition provide distinct measurements of age-associated biological variation. This project implements a prediction-level integration framework comprising a blood methylation regressor, a skin microbiome regressor, and a late-fusion operator. Public datasets included 360 discovery blood samples, 274 external blood samples, and 1,798 skin samples representing 339 participant identifiers. Nested validation yielded mean absolute errors (MAEs) of 4.21 years for blood methylation and 9.17 years for skin microbiome prediction. External blood validation yielded an MAE of 6.03 years. The modality-specific regressors were fitted on Modal and combined through a prespecified equal-weight prediction rule. Because the available cohorts do not contain verified paired blood and skin specimens, joint predictive accuracy and improvement over single-modality models remain unestimated.

1. Introduction

The 2023 W3PHIAI abstract proposed chronological-age estimation through late fusion of blood methylation and skin microbiome predictors. The present implementation follows that architecture while evaluating its component models using reproducible public-data analyses. It is a new computational study conducted in 2026; it does not recover the original code, datasets, or results.

The biological rationale is that methylation and microbial composition may encode complementary age-associated information. Whether their combination improves prediction depends on the accuracy, bias, and within-person error dependence of the component models. Accordingly, component validation, data-quality assessment, and paired evaluation of the fusion estimator are distinct methodological requirements. Chronological-age prediction is the endpoint; biological-age estimation and clinical utility are not evaluated.

2. Methods

2.1 Datasets and eligibility criteria

Blood methylation discovery data were obtained from GSE41037. Of 720 deposited samples, 360 were retained after restricting to healthy controls aged at least 18 years with a positive source quality-control flag. External validation used 274 control women from GSE19711. Both processed matrices contained 27,578 CpG identifiers. The external target was age at recruitment, which may differ from age at blood collection.

Skin microbial abundances and metadata were obtained from the Huang et al. repository. The released matrix contained 1,975 samples and 7,311 amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Study 11052 was excluded because all 177 samples shared one participant identifier despite discrepant age values. The retained dataset comprised 1,798 samples and 339 study-qualified participant identifiers across three studies. Thirteen retained identifiers had one-year differences in recorded age across samples; all samples associated with an identifier remained in the same validation fold.

Skin observations were weighted inversely by the number of samples per participant during fitting and primary evaluation. This prevented participants with repeated sampling from contributing disproportionate weight. Dataset composition and age distributions are reported in Figure 1. The source microbial matrix had undergone age-independent prevalence filtering before release; that upstream processing could not be reconstructed from the available table.

2.2 Modality-specific age prediction

The methylation pipeline comprised median imputation, age-correlation feature selection, standardization, and ridge regression. Five outer folds held out complete assay plates. Three-fold plate-grouped inner validation selected 100 or 500 CpGs and ridge regularization parameters α ∈ {10, 100, 1000}. Every preprocessing operation was fitted within the relevant training partition. A final discovery-only selection identified 500 CpGs and α = 100; the resulting model was applied to the external controls without age-based recalibration.

The microbiome pipeline applied a sample-local Hellinger transformation, equivalent to the square root of relative abundance, and a training-only prevalence threshold of 1%. Random forests contained 160 trees with square-root feature subsampling. Minimum leaf sizes of 1, 3, and 5 were compared using three-fold participant-grouped inner validation within five outer participant-grouped folds. A minimum leaf size of 1 was selected in every outer fold and used for the final component refit. Final refitting does not provide an additional estimate of prediction accuracy.

2.3 Late-fusion architecture

The integrated estimator combines the component predictions for a shared participant:

ŷfusion = w ŷblood + (1 − w) ŷskin,   w = 0.5.

Equal weighting is a prespecified implementation choice. It was not optimized using the unpaired cohorts. The inference interface requires matched participant indices, documented specimen linkage, and the fitted feature schemas. Missing, duplicated, or discordant participant identifiers are rejected. Independent subjects are not matched by age or by row order. Separate blood-only and skin-only inference methods are available when only one modality is measured.

The model artifact contains both fitted regressors, their feature identifiers, and the fusion operator. Fitting was executed on Modal using four CPU cores; the selected estimators did not require GPU execution. Serialization checks confirmed preservation of predictions. The refitted blood model differed from the previously recorded external predictions by at most 0.00014 years, consistent with numerical differences between compute environments. No paired test participants were available, so the model manifest records joint accuracy as unestimated.

2.4 Validation and statistical analysis

Training-age median baselines were evaluated for both modalities. A skin metadata-only ridge model used study, body site, and sex. A fixed random forest with minimum leaf size 3 was evaluated using random-sample, participant-held-out, and leave-one-study-out splits to assess the effects of sampling structure. Ten label-permutation runs per modality were compared with matched unshuffled models; skin permutation operated on participant-mean ages.

MAE, root mean squared error, and R² were computed from held-out predictions. MAE intervals used 2,000 bootstrap resamples of assay plates for internal blood validation, samples for external blood validation, and participants for skin validation. These intervals condition on fitted predictions and do not include uncertainty from retraining. The random seed was 20260905. Comparisons across modalities are descriptive because the cohorts differ in demographic composition and age range.

Blood methylation prediction achieved R² = 0.91 during plate-held-out validation. External validation in control women aged 52–78 years yielded MAE = 6.03 years and R² = -1.38. Negative external R² indicates higher squared error than prediction by the external-cohort mean. The difference between internal and external results therefore limits evidence for generalization despite improvement over the discovery-training median baseline.

The skin microbiome model yielded MAE = 9.17 years, compared with 10.06 years for the study/site/sex model. The paired participant-bootstrap reduction was 0.89 years (95% conditional interval, 0.46–1.32). Microbial composition contributed predictive information in this validation setting, although metadata accounted for a substantial fraction of the observed signal.

3.2 Sampling structure and external validity

Random sample splitting placed participants represented in the training set in 90.5% of test samples. Participant and study holdout eliminated this overlap. For the fixed forest, participant-weighted MAE increased from 8.93 years under random splitting to 9.24 years under participant holdout. The paired difference was 0.31 years (95% conditional interval, 0.15–0.48). For identical participant-held-out predictions, sample-weighted MAE was 6.44 years, demonstrating sensitivity to the evaluation weights.

Leave-one-study-out MAE was 18.35 years. Study 1841 predominantly included younger adults, whereas study 10317 covered a broader and older population. Differences in age distribution and study composition are therefore plausible contributors to the reduced transfer performance. These analyses use different eligibility criteria, weighting, transformations, and model settings from the source publication and do not constitute a direct reproduction of its reported accuracy.

3.3 Assay quality and feature interpretation

Two external blood samples had more than 1% missing CpG measurements. The most incomplete sample had approximately 57% missing measurements and a predicted age of −28.4 years at recorded age 74. Both samples were retained in the primary analysis. An exploratory 1% missingness filter applied to the existing predictions retained 272 controls and yielded MAE = 5.48 years and R² = −0.26. This post hoc sensitivity analysis identifies a quality-control concern without establishing external validity.

CpGs annotated to NHLRC1, SCGN, and ASPA had large standardized ridge coefficients and were selected in all five outer training sets. These conditional coefficients do not identify causal aging mechanisms. Microbial training-importance ranks included ASVs assigned to Mycoplasma and Enterobacteriaceae; interpretation is limited by feature correlation, potential study effects, and the resolution of the released taxonomic annotations.

Matched unshuffled models had lower MAE than the ten corresponding label-permutation runs in each modality. This diagnostic supports learnable age-associated structure in the selected datasets, but the number of permutations is insufficient for a precise significance estimate.

4. Discussion

This implementation establishes the two fitted component regressors and prediction-level fusion architecture specified by the original project concept. The component results support age prediction within the selected datasets, with weaker performance under external evaluation. They do not establish that fusion improves accuracy.

For component errors eb and es, fusion error variance satisfies:

Var(efusion) = w² Var(eb) + (1 − w)² Var(es) + 2w(1 − w) Cov(eb, es).

The unpaired cohorts do not identify the within-person covariance term or joint bias. Consequently, separate component error estimates cannot be combined to infer empirical fusion performance. A paired cohort would permit weight estimation on training or calibration data and comparison of the locked fusion model against both components on the same untouched test participants.

Additional limitations include processed-input dependence, ambiguity in a subset of source identifiers, a restricted external age range, potential assay and study effects, and the distinction between chronological and biological age. The original abstract proposed a larger methylation resource exceeding 12,000 profiles; the present implementation uses a smaller explicitly curated discovery cohort and independent validation cohort. Neither that original sample size nor the abstract’s anticipated fusion advantage is claimed as an executed result.

Serialized models should be loaded only from trusted sources using the recorded package versions.

Supply feature matrices indexed by verified shared participant/visit identifiers; predict_paired returns the two component predictions and their prespecified weighted average. The provided public datasets do not satisfy the paired-input requirement.
