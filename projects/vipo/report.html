Most reasoning failures I see in small language models are not illegal steps. They are perfectly legal steps that quietly delete the only remaining path to the answer. A terminal reward cannot tell the model which step did that. This project asks what happens when the reward is placed exactly at that step instead, and answers it with a proof, a training run, two ablations, and a failure mode.

1. The problem: legal is not the same as alive

Countdown gives a model a few numbers, say [3, 4, 6, 8], and a target, say 24. It must combine the numbers with +, −, ×, ÷, each number used once, until one value equals the target. Every step removes two numbers and adds their result, so the state is just the multiset of what is left. A step like 8 − 6 = 2 is arithmetically fine and looks like progress, and from [2, 3, 4] the target is still reachable (2 × 3 × 4). The step 8 + 6 = 14 is exactly as legal and looks exactly as reasonable, and from [3, 4, 14] no combination reaches 24 at all. Whether a step was good depends on a question the model is never asked: after taking it, does a solution still exist? One of those two moves ended the game two steps before the final answer was written down, and nothing in the trace says which.

Supervised fine-tuning imitates one demonstration and never sees the alternatives. DPO and IPO compare two complete responses. RLOO scores the final answer with a verifier. All three place the learning signal on the whole trace, so when a six-step trace fails because step two killed the search space, the gradient has to guess which step to blame. On a branching problem that guess is statistically expensive, and I could prove how expensive.

2. The idea: reward the transition, not the trace

The state graph of Countdown is finite and small. That makes a quantity computable that is usually only estimable: whether a state is still solvable. Define the residual horizon of a state \(s\) for target \(y\) by dynamic programming over multisets of rationals,

\[ d_y(s)=\begin{cases}0 & s\ni y\\ 1+\min_{a\in\mathcal A(s)} d_y(T(s,a)) & |s|>1\\ \infty & |s|=1,\ s\not\ni y\end{cases}\qquad \mathrm{Alive}_y(s)=\mathbf 1[d_y(s)<\infty]. \]

The viability label of an action is simply the aliveness of the state it leads to, \(z_y(s,a)=\mathrm{Alive}_y(T(s,a))\), and the per-step reward is 1 for an alive step, a small \(\rho=0.1\) for a legal-but-dead step, and 0 for anything unparseable or arithmetically wrong. Exact rational arithmetic makes division unambiguous, and memoization makes the oracle essentially free: a typical solvable 4-number instance has about twenty alive states in total.

Training is self-play with a group-relative policy gradient. At each state the policy samples \(G=8\) candidate next steps, the oracle labels each, and the leave-one-out advantage \(A_i=r_i-\frac{1}{G-1}\sum_{j\ne i}r_j\) drives

\[ \mathcal L(\theta)=-\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^G A_i\log\pi_\theta(\hat a_i\mid p(s,h))+\lambda_{\mathrm{KL}}\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})-\lambda_{\mathrm{ent}}\widehat H(\pi_\theta). \]

The update is the same shape as RLOO. What changed is only what is being rewarded: a transition property instead of a terminal score. The rollout then advances along a sampled alive step (or, early in training when the policy cannot find one, an oracle-supplied alive step, never at test time) so that later states are visited from the start.

3. What can be proven

Because the oracle is exact, the labels come with guarantees rather than reward-model error.

  • Sound and complete. \(z_y(s,a)=1\) if and only if a legal continuation after \(a\) reaches the goal. This is the definition unwound: the continuation is exactly the witness.
  • Staying alive solves. If the start state is alive and a deterministic policy chooses an alive action at every nonterminal state, it reaches the goal in at most \(m-1\) steps. Each action shrinks the multiset by one, every reached state is alive by induction, and an alive single-element state can only be the target. The corollary is the sharp one: a dead transition is irrecoverable, no future policy can save the instance.
  • Exponential credit-assignment separation. On a depth-\(H\), branching-\(B\) search where exactly one action stays alive at each level and reward is given only at the end, deciding whether a fixed root action is alive from terminal-only random rollouts needs \(N=\Omega\!\left(B^{H-1}\log(1/\delta)\right)\) samples to see a single success with probability \(1-\delta\). One oracle query decides it. The proof is two lines: the success probability of a random continuation is \(p=B^{-(H-1)}\) and \((1-p)^N\le\delta\) forces \(N\ge\log(1/\delta)/(-\log(1-p))=\Theta(p^{-1}\log(1/\delta))\).

4. Setup: one seed, one verifier, two budgets

Every method starts from the same SFT seed, a Qwen2.5-0.5B fine-tuned on Countdown demonstrations, and is scored by the same rule-based evaluator (1.0 for a valid equation using each number once, 0.1 for well-formed but wrong, 0.0 for unparseable). VPO trains for 200 self-play updates (batch 8, \(G=8\), lr \(10^{-5}\), \(\lambda_{\mathrm{KL}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{ent}}=10^{-3}\), temperature 0.8) on one H100, alternating a vLLM sampling worker and a PyTorch update worker that hand off through on-disk checkpoints because they cannot share the GPU. Evaluation is on a fresh held-out set of 500 problems with 16 rollouts per prompt, reporting Pass@k with 95 % Wilson intervals.

The same checkpoint is evaluated two ways. VPO-A samples candidate steps and takes the first legal one, with no oracle at test time: this is what the policy learned. VPO-R samples candidates and lets the oracle's viability label pick an alive one: this is a diagnostic upper bound on how much signal the label carries at inference, not a second model.

Because the two rewards give different kinds of feedback, I tracked two cost budgets instead of one: the number of reward labels (oracle or verifier calls) and the number of training output tokens generated. RLOO: about 102,400 labels and 50.7 M tokens. VPO: about 64,000 labels and 1.54 M tokens, because a labeled sample is a single step string rather than a full answer.

6. Ablations: where the gain comes from, and where it breaks

Why does the aggressive repair rule blow up? My reading is that it changes the sampling distribution of the group away from the policy just where the advantage is computed, so the leave-one-out baseline is contaminated by actions the policy did not produce, and late in training, when most candidates are already alive and the occasional bad one triggers a replacement, the gradient starts pushing probability toward tokens the policy never emitted. It is the same lesson as importance-weight blow-ups in off-policy RL, arriving through a side door.

7. Investigating the premise: how hazardous is Countdown, really?

The whole method rests on the claim that legal-but-dead moves are common and hard to spot. I wanted a number, so I reimplemented the residual oracle from scratch (exact rational DP over canonicalized multisets, memoized) and ran it over 3,000 uniformly random 4-number instances with numbers and targets in 1–99. The script and its output are next to this page.

That last number is the one that matters. "Get closer to the target" is precisely the heuristic a fluent model learns from demonstrations, and it kills the instance more often than not. A few of the cases the analysis pulled out:

8. Implementation notes

The oracle (countdown_oracle.py) exposes min_steps_to_target, is_alive and action_labels over canonicalized rational multisets. A step environment (countdown_step_env.py) parses the model's <step>a op b = c</step> strings, checks the arithmetic and the number usage, and attaches alive, distance_after and error metadata. Training alternates a vLLM sampling worker and a grouped policy-update worker; they cannot share the GPU, so the orchestrator kills one before creating the other and hands state across through checkpoints on disk. KL and entropy are estimated from sampled tokens only, since forming full vocabulary distributions over prompt tokens ran out of memory, and the loss supervises generated step tokens only.

The RLOO and IPO baselines are my own implementations too, built as milestones before VPO: IPO trained one epoch on 48k preference pairs with \(\beta=0.05\) (chosen over 0.1 because the smaller value pushes the loss target from 5 to 10 and measurably improved Pass@1), and RLOO for 100 steps of 128 prompts × 8 rollouts with a clipped sequence-level importance weight. Both were validated on the milestone test set before being used as comparators.

9. Limitations, stated plainly

  • VPO changes the reward interface, so it does not receive identical feedback to RLOO. The defensible claim is the narrower one: placing the reward at the step where solvability is decided gives a more accurate and much cheaper model on this task, which is why both the label budget and the token budget are reported rather than one number.
  • VPO-R uses the oracle at test time; it is an upper bound on what the viability signal can provide at inference. VPO-A is the fair comparison against SFT and IPO, and it does not beat RLOO on Pass@1.
  • Countdown admits an exact oracle. Elsewhere the alive predicate would come from a simulator, a constraint solver, or a learned critic, and the soundness theorem then holds only to the extent the approximation does.
  • The model is 0.5B parameters and the task is small; the results are about credit assignment, not about scale.

10. What I would do next

  • Approximate oracles. Replace the DP with a learned value head trained on oracle labels and measure how fast the theorems degrade as its accuracy drops; this is the bridge to tasks without an exact reachability predicate.
  • Distill the reranker into the policy. VPO-R shows the signal is there at inference; a second training phase that supervises the policy on the reranker's choices should close the 0.49 → 0.88 gap without an oracle at test time.
  • Harder state spaces. Sokoban-style puzzles and program synthesis with a type checker have exactly the legal-but-dead structure, with a cheap partial oracle.
  • Explain the repair collapse. The off-policy contamination story above is a hypothesis; an experiment that logs the importance ratio of inserted actions would settle it.
