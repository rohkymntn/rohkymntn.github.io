Sequential reasoning models frequently generate locally valid operations that irreversibly remove every path to a correct solution, while terminal rewards provide no direct evidence about the transition responsible for failure. We developed viability-guided policy optimization, which assigns step-level reward according to exact reachability of the target state, and evaluated its theoretical credit-assignment properties, training efficiency, predictive performance and characteristic failure modes on Countdown arithmetic.

1. Failure localization in sequential arithmetic reasoning

Countdown requires a model to combine a finite set of numbers with addition, subtraction, multiplication and division until the target value is obtained, using each number once. Because every operation replaces two values with their result, arithmetically valid transitions can be divided into viable moves that preserve at least one solution and irreversible moves that render the target unreachable, even when both appear locally plausible from the numerical distance to the target.

Supervised fine-tuning observes one demonstrated trajectory, direct preference optimization and identity preference optimization compare complete responses and reinforcement learning with leave-one-out baselines assigns verifier reward to the terminal answer. Each approach therefore distributes learning signal across the full trace, leaving the optimization procedure to infer which earlier transition eliminated the remaining solution set.

2. Viability-guided policy optimization

The finite Countdown state graph permits exact computation of whether a target remains reachable from any intermediate state, allowing the residual solution horizon to be defined by dynamic programming over canonical multisets of rational numbers.

\[ d_y(s)=\begin{cases}0 & s\ni y\\ 1+\min_{a\in\mathcal A(s)} d_y(T(s,a)) & |s|>1\\ \infty & |s|=1,\ s\not\ni y\end{cases}\qquad \mathrm{Alive}_y(s)=\mathbf 1[d_y(s)<\infty]. \]

An action receives a viability label from the reachability of its successor state and a reward of 1 for a viable transition, 0.1 for a legal but irreversible transition and 0 for an unparsable or arithmetically invalid output. Exact rational arithmetic removes ambiguity from division, while memoization reduces the cost of oracle evaluation because a typical solvable four-number instance contains approximately twenty viable states.

Training uses self-play with a group-relative policy gradient in which the policy samples eight candidate transitions at each state, the oracle assigns viability rewards and a leave-one-out advantage weights the log probability of each sampled action.

\[ \mathcal L(\theta)=-\frac1G\sum_{i=1}^G A_i\log\pi_\theta(\hat a_i\mid p(s,h))+\lambda_{\mathrm{KL}}\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})-\lambda_{\mathrm{ent}}\widehat H(\pi_\theta). \]

The optimization objective retains the group-relative form of RLOO while replacing terminal verification with a transition-level reachability signal. Rollouts advance through a sampled viable action, with an oracle-supplied viable transition used during early training only when the policy produces none, thereby exposing the model to later reasoning states without using the oracle during policy-only evaluation.

3. Theoretical properties

Exact reachability labels support formal guarantees for action validity, convergence under viability-preserving policies and the sample complexity separating transition supervision from terminal reward.

  • Soundness and completeness. The label \(z_y(s,a)=1\) holds if and only if at least one legal continuation from the successor state reaches the target, making the continuation a constructive witness of viability.
  • Convergence under viable actions. From a viable initial state, a deterministic policy that selects a viable action at every nonterminal state reaches the target within at most \(m-1\) transitions because each operation reduces the multiset cardinality by one and preserves reachability. A transition to a nonviable state is consequently irreversible.
  • Exponential separation in credit assignment. In a depth-\(H\) search tree with branching factor \(B\), one viable action at each depth and terminal-only reward, identifying a viable root action through random continuation requires \(N=\Omega\!\left(B^{H-1}\log(1/\delta)\right)\) samples to observe a success with probability \(1-\delta\). One exact reachability query determines the same label because the random-continuation probability is \(p=B^{-(H-1)}\) and \((1-p)^N\le\delta\) implies \(N\ge\log(1/\delta)/(-\log(1-p))=\Theta(p^{-1}\log(1/\delta))\).

4. Experimental design and computational budget

All methods were initialized from the same Qwen2.5-0.5B model fine-tuned on Countdown demonstrations and evaluated by an identical rule-based scorer that assigned 1.0 to a valid equation using every number once, 0.1 to a well-formed but incorrect expression and 0 to an unparsable response. Viability-guided optimization used 200 self-play updates on one H100 with batches of eight states, eight candidates per state, a learning rate of 10-5, KL and entropy coefficients of 10-3 and a sampling temperature of 0.8, followed by evaluation on 500 held-out problems with 16 rollouts per prompt and 95 percent Wilson intervals.

The trained checkpoint was evaluated as VPO-A, which selected the first legal candidate without oracle access, and as VPO-R, which used the reachability oracle to select a viable candidate from the same sampled set. VPO-A measures the policy acquired during training, whereas VPO-R estimates the performance attainable when viability information is retained during inference.

Computational efficiency was evaluated using both the number of reward evaluations and the number of generated training tokens because transition and terminal supervision operate at different sequence lengths. RLOO used approximately 102,400 verifier evaluations and 50.7 million generated tokens, compared with approximately 64,000 oracle evaluations and 1.54 million generated tokens for viability-guided optimization.

5.1 Training dynamics

6. Ablation analysis

Aggressive repair changes the group sampling distribution at the point where the leave-one-out advantage is calculated by introducing actions not generated by the current policy. As the viable-action rate increases, infrequent replacements can therefore assign gradients to off-policy tokens with uncorrected importance ratios, providing a plausible mechanism for the late optimization instability observed in the ablation.

7. Prevalence of irreversible transitions in Countdown

The prevalence of irreversible legal transitions was quantified using an independently implemented exact rational dynamic program applied to 3,000 uniformly sampled four-number instances with operands and targets between 1 and 99.

The failure of the numerically closest transition in 53 percent of sampled instances shows that immediate proximity to the target is poorly aligned with preservation of the global solution set, despite its plausibility as a heuristic learned from successful demonstrations. 8. Implementation

The oracle in countdown_oracle.py computes the minimum solution horizon, state viability and transition labels over canonical rational multisets, while countdown_step_env.py parses generated operations, verifies arithmetic and operand use and records viability and residual-distance metadata. Training alternates between vLLM sampling and grouped PyTorch optimization through on-disk checkpoints because the workers cannot occupy the GPU simultaneously, with KL divergence and entropy estimated from sampled tokens and loss restricted to generated transition tokens.

Comparator implementations were developed and validated before the viability experiments, with IPO trained for one epoch on 48,000 preference pairs using \(\beta=0.05\) and RLOO trained for 100 updates of 128 prompts with eight rollouts and a clipped sequence-level importance weight. Both methods were evaluated on the milestone test set before inclusion in the final comparison.

9. Limitations

  • Viability-guided optimization and RLOO receive rewards at different temporal resolutions, requiring computational comparison through both reward evaluations and generated tokens rather than a single feedback count.
  • VPO-R retains oracle access during inference and therefore estimates performance with reachability-guided selection, whereas policy-only VPO-A provides the direct comparison with supervised fine-tuning and IPO and remains below RLOO on Pass@1.
  • Exact reachability is available for Countdown, while extension to domains that require simulation, constraint solving or a learned critic will depend on the calibration and error structure of the approximate viability estimator.
  • The experiments use a 0.5-billion-parameter model and a finite arithmetic task, leaving the behaviour of viability supervision at larger model and state-space scales unresolved.

10. Future directions

  • Approximate viability estimation. A learned value model trained from exact oracle labels could quantify how reachability error affects policy optimization and establish operating thresholds for tasks without an exact state predicate.
  • Policy distillation. Supervision from oracle-selected candidates could transfer the inference-time advantage of VPO-R into the policy and test whether the observed Pass@1 difference can be recovered without oracle access.
  • Higher-dimensional state spaces. Sokoban-style planning and type-constrained program synthesis provide larger search graphs in which locally valid actions can irreversibly eliminate the goal while partial viability checks remain computationally accessible.
  • Mechanistic analysis of repair instability. Logging the importance ratios and gradient contributions of inserted teacher actions would test whether off-policy contamination explains the observed late-stage collapse.
